THE SECOND IDEA IS THE ESSAY'S SPINE, and it is publishable as an ARGUMENT even though its source is
confidential — because the argument is general and needs no program, metric, or artifact to stand up.
Strip the setting entirely. Do not describe the internal project it came from. Make the case abstractly

THE ARGUMENT TO MAKE, roughly:
  * Cheap production has made model access ordinary. The scarce thing is the structure around it.
  * A specific, uncomfortable case: sometimes you cannot measure which approach is better, not because
    you lack rigour but because the setup forecloses a fair test. Holding out data is what makes
    comparison possible, and there are real situations where holding anything out is the wrong call.
  * When that happens, teams tend to do one of two bad things: manufacture a metric that looks like
    evidence but is not, or defer the decision indefinitely for want of one.
  * The honest third option is to make the argument explicitly, in writing, before the fact — say what
    you are choosing and why, and what would change your mind. That is a strategic decision, not a
    modelling one, and pretending otherwise is where the dishonesty enters.
  * This connects to the expert-judgment thesis: the reason the operating layer matters is that expert
    judgment is exactly what you fall back on when measurement is unavailable, and a system that
    flattens it has removed its own safety net.

REQUIREMENTS:
  * 1200-1800 words. It must read as an argument someone could disagree with, not a description of
    projects. If a paragraph could be replaced by "here is a thing I built," cut it.
  * Athan's voice, first person. The corpus shows he states a position and names the condition that
    would revise it — do that here explicitly at least once. That construction is the essay's

  * Concrete without being confidential: use public, general examples (held-out test sets,
