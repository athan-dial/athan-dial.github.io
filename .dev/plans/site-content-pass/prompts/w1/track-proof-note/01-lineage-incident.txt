THE STORY: a one-character naming error — a plural where the schema had a singular — meant a boolean
flag evaluated false for every row. It propagated silently through several layers of a data pipeline
into a field that downstream consumers read as truth, and then into a workflow that spent real money
on the strength of it. It surfaced only when someone queried for records they KNEW existed and got
none back. The fix was a rename, deployed within days.

TWO PRECISION CORRECTIONS YOU MUST GET RIGHT — they are the reason this piece is credible:
  1. The cost is APPROXIMATE, not exact. The source says "approximately". Never write it as an exact
     figure. And per the safety contract, consider whether to give the figure at all or characterise
     it — a small, almost trivial sum is the rhetorically stronger framing anyway, because the point
     is that the amount was tiny and the failure was structural.
  2. The early count of AFFECTED records and the later count of PURCHASED records are DIFFERENT
     DENOMINATORS and must not be presented as one number. The early figure was an estimate during
     investigation; the later one is the settled purchase impact. If you mention both, make the
     distinction explicit. This is precisely the kind of sloppiness the whole evidence gate exists to
     catch, so getting it right in the prose is part of the argument.

WHAT MAKES IT WORTH PUBLISHING: every individual layer behaved correctly. Nothing threw an error. The
validation that existed could not distinguish "this column is all false" from "this column is
legitimately all false," and the only thing that caught it was a human with domain knowledge noticing
an impossible result. That is a real claim about verification in data systems, and it generalises well
beyond this incident — which is what earns it a place on the site.
